What is computer vision? A plain guide for business owners

Computer vision lets software understand images and video. Here is how it works, what it can do for a business, where it struggles and how to start small.

Computer vision is the part of artificial intelligence that lets software understand images and video. It is how a phone unlocks when it sees your face, how a car park reads number plates and how a camera can tell a person from a passing shadow.

You do not need to understand the mathematics to use it well. You do need a clear picture of what it can and cannot do. This guide gives you that.

How a computer "sees"

To a computer, a picture is a grid of numbers. Each tiny dot, or pixel, has values for how red, green and blue it is. A single camera frame contains a very large number of them, and a video adds many frames every second.

A computer vision model learns to find patterns in those numbers. It is trained by showing it many examples with the right answer attached: "this is a person", "this box is damaged", "this is a car". Over time, it learns which patterns of pixels match which answers. When it sees a new image, it uses what it learned to make its best guess, along with a score for how confident it is.

That last point is important. Computer vision does not "know" things the way people do. It recognises patterns similar to what it has seen before. When reality looks very different from its training examples, it can be confidently wrong.

What computer vision can do

Most business uses are built from a handful of basic tasks:

  • Classification: what is in this picture? A ripe or unripe fruit, a good or faulty part.
  • Detection: where are the objects? It draws a box around each person, vehicle or product.
  • Tracking: following the same object from frame to frame, so you can see where it goes.
  • Counting: how many people entered, how many items are on a shelf.
  • Segmentation: outlining the exact shape of something, such as a crack or a spill.
  • Reading text: number plates, labels, meter readings and documents.
  • Recognising actions: what is happening over a few seconds, such as someone falling or a fight starting.

Where businesses use it

Once you know the building blocks, you start to see uses everywhere:

  • Retail: security alerts, counting visitors, spotting empty shelves.
  • Manufacturing: inspecting products on a line, checking that safety equipment is worn.
  • Warehouses: reading labels and tracking stock as it moves.
  • Agriculture: checking crops and livestock from cameras or drones.
  • Buildings and homes: intrusion alerts, monitoring entrances, night-time surveillance.
  • Transport and parking: reading number plates, counting vehicles, finding free spaces.

What it needs to work well

Successful computer vision projects tend to have four things in common:

  1. A clear, narrow question. "Alert me when someone enters the stockroom after 10 pm" is a good question. "Make my shop safer" is not, yet.
  2. Good images. The right camera position and enough light matter more than expensive hardware.
  3. The right examples. The model must have learned from situations that look like yours.
  4. Honest testing. It should be tested on footage from the real site, including ordinary moments where nothing happens.

Its limits

Computer vision struggles with things people handle easily: very poor light, heavy rain or fog, objects that are mostly hidden, and situations it has never seen. It can also raise false alarms when something harmless looks unusual. Good systems are designed around these limits, for example by double-checking an event before alerting anyone.

There are also responsibilities. Cameras capture people, so privacy matters. Processing video on site, keeping only what you need and being open about where cameras are installed are good habits in any country.

Where it runs

A computer vision model can run in the cloud or on a small device next to the camera. Running it on site keeps video private and lets the system react without waiting for the internet. We explain the choice in Edge AI vs cloud AI: when to use which.

How to start

Start small. Pick one problem that costs you time or money, collect some sample footage, and build a small prototype to see how it performs in your conditions. If it works, expand. If it does not, you have learned that cheaply.
