How AI cameras turn video into alerts: a guide for shop owners

What happens between a camera seeing something and your phone buzzing, in plain English, and what to ask before you buy an AI camera for your shop.

A normal CCTV system records. The footage is useful after something has happened: you rewind, you find the moment, you share it with the police or your insurer. An AI camera tries to do something different. It tries to tell you while it is happening.

That sounds like magic, but it is a chain of simple steps. Once you understand them, you can judge any AI camera on the market, including ours, much more clearly.

Step 1: the camera captures video

A camera does not record "a video" in the way we think of it. It takes many still pictures every second, called frames. Everything the AI knows comes from these frames, so the camera itself still matters a lot.

More megapixels are rarely the answer. What usually makes the biggest difference is where the camera points and how the scene is lit:

  • Cover the places where problems actually happen: entrances, tills, high-value shelves and blind corners.
  • Avoid pointing a camera straight at a bright window or door. The people in front of it turn into dark shapes.
  • Mount cameras high enough that shelves and customers do not block the view.

Step 2: the AI looks at short clips, not single photos

A single photo of a customer holding a product tells you almost nothing. Are they reading the label, putting it in a basket or hiding it in a jacket? The difference is in the movement, and movement only shows up over time.

That is why modern systems look at short clips of a few seconds. The AI first finds the people and objects in the frames, then looks at how they move across the clip. It has learned what different actions look like from many labelled examples, in the same way a new employee learns what suspicious behaviour looks like by being shown it.

Step 3: deciding whether an event is worth an alert

For every clip, the AI produces something like a score: how strongly does this look like theft, or like a fight? The system then compares that score with a threshold.

Setting the threshold is a real trade-off. Set it too low and you get an alert every time someone bends down quickly. Set it too high and you miss things. Good systems also let you narrow things down with simple rules, such as only watching a stockroom door after closing time, or ignoring the area behind the counter where staff work.

Step 4: checking before alerting

Here is where many systems fall short. A single AI decision will sometimes be wrong. A shadow, a reflection or a child running down an aisle can look unusual for a moment. If each of those becomes a notification on your phone, you will soon start ignoring the alerts, and then the system has failed even when it works.

One effective fix is to add a second look. When the first stage flags an event, a second AI check looks at the same moment again, more carefully, before anything is sent. Only events that pass both checks reach you.

From our PiLENS tests: A second AI check cut false alerts on normal test videos from 16.4% to 3.6%.

Step 5: the alert reaches you

A useful alert is more than a buzz. It should tell you which camera, what time and what the system thinks it saw, ideally with a short clip or snapshot so you can judge it in seconds. Then the decision is yours: check the floor, speak to a staff member, or call for help.

An AI camera does not replace your judgement. It gives you something you cannot have otherwise: attention on every camera, all the time, even when you are busy serving customers.

Where the AI runs matters

All of the steps above can happen in one of two places. Some systems send your video to servers in the cloud for analysis. Others run the AI on a small device inside your shop, next to the camera.

Running on site means your footage does not have to leave the building, alerts keep working if the internet drops, and you are not paying to upload video all day. Cloud systems can be easier to manage across many branches. We compare the two in more detail in Edge AI vs cloud AI: when to use which.

Questions to ask before you buy

Whoever you buy from, these questions will tell you a lot:

  1. Where is my video processed and stored? On site, in the cloud, or both?
  2. How do you handle false alarms? Ask how they measured them, and on what kind of footage.
  3. Will it work in my lighting? Ask to test it on footage from your own shop.
  4. What happens when the internet is down?
  5. What will it cost each month, not just on day one?
  6. Can I adjust it? Zones, schedules and alert settings should be easy to change.

If a seller promises perfect accuracy or no false alarms at all, treat that as a warning sign. Every AI system makes mistakes. The good ones are honest about how often, and are designed to keep those mistakes away from you.
